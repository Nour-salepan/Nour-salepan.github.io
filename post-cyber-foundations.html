Cybersecurity Foundations, Explained Simply

Everything in Course 1 of the Google Cybersecurity Certificate — the CIA triad, the eight security domains, how attacks actually work, and what a security analyst does all day — written the way I wish someone had explained it to me.

Cybersecurity has a jargon problem. Walk into it cold and you'll meet CIA triads, CISSP domains, NIST frameworks and threat actors inside the first hour — and most explanations assume you already know what they mean.

I'm working through the Google Cybersecurity Professional Certificate, and this is my write-up of Course 1: Foundations of Cybersecurity. Not a summary of the slides — an attempt to explain each concept the way it finally made sense to me, with the analogies that made it click.

If you're considering this field, this is roughly the mental model you need before anything technical will land.

Part 01What cybersecurity actually means

The textbook definition: cybersecurity is the practice of ensuring confidentiality, integrity and availability of information by protecting networks, devices, people and data from unauthorised access or criminal exploitation.

That's accurate and almost useless on a first read. Here's the version that helped me.

Security is the business of keeping promises about information. When a hospital stores your medical record, it has implicitly promised three things: that only the right people can read it, that nobody can secretly alter it, and that your doctor can get to it when you're in front of them bleeding. Every security control that exists — firewalls, passwords, backups, encryption, the lot — is a mechanism for keeping one of those three promises.

Attacks are promise-breaking. A data breach breaks the first. Tampering with records breaks the second. Ransomware breaks the third. Once I saw it this way, the rest of the field stopped feeling like a list of unrelated topics.

Why anyone pays for this

Breaches cost money, customers and legal standing. Organisations hire security people for the same reason they hire accountants — not because it's exciting, but because the alternative is catastrophic and the regulators are watching.

Part 02The CIA triad — the whole field in three words

Nothing to do with the intelligence agency. It's the three properties you're defending, and essentially every security decision traces back to one of them.

PropertyThe promiseBroken when…Protected by
Confidentiality Only authorised people can see it Customer records leak online Access controls, encryption, least privilege
Integrity The data is correct and unaltered An attacker silently edits a bank balance Hashing, checksums, audit logs, version control
Availability Authorised people can reach it when needed Ransomware locks every file Backups, redundancy, DDoS protection

The part that makes this genuinely useful rather than a poster on a wall: the three pull against each other. Maximum confidentiality means nobody can read anything, which destroys availability. Maximum availability means no barriers at all, which destroys confidentiality. Security work is permanently a negotiation between them, and the right balance depends entirely on what you're protecting.

A public train timetable is nearly all availability — who cares who reads it, but it had better be reachable and correct. A list of undercover informants is nearly all confidentiality — better unreachable than leaked. Same framework, opposite settings.

If someone proposes a control, ask which letter it protects and which letter it costs.

Part 03Four attacks that changed everything

The history isn't trivia. Each of these forced a permanent change in how systems get built, and you can still see their fingerprints in today's defaults.

1986

The Brain virus

Written by two brothers in Pakistan to track piracy of their medical software, it spread worldwide via floppy disks — far beyond anything intended. It wasn't destructive, but it proved code could propagate between machines on its own. Lesson: intent doesn't limit blast radius.

1988

The Morris worm

A graduate student's experiment to measure the size of the internet. A flaw in its logic meant it reinfected machines repeatedly until they collapsed under the load, taking down a significant share of the early internet. It led directly to the creation of coordinated incident response teams (CERTs). Lesson: availability is a security property, and bugs in your own tools are a threat.

2000

LoveLetter (ILOVEYOU)

An email with the subject "I love you" and an attachment. Opening it stole credentials and mailed itself to the victim's entire address book. It hit tens of millions of machines. This is the moment the industry really understood social engineering — the attack didn't break a system, it persuaded a person.

2017

The Equifax breach

Roughly 147 million people's personal and financial data exposed, through a known vulnerability that had a patch available and wasn't applied. Lesson: most real breaches aren't exotic. They're basic hygiene that someone didn't do.

The pattern

Two of those four were enabled by human behaviour, and one by an unapplied patch. The glamorous image of security — a genius defeating cryptography — is almost never what actually happens. The boring controls are the ones that matter.

Part 04How attackers actually get in

A few terms first, because they get used interchangeably and shouldn't be.

  • Threat — anything that could cause harm. A flood is a threat.
  • Vulnerability — a weakness that a threat could exploit. A building with no flood barriers.
  • Risk — the combination: how likely, and how bad. A flood threat plus no barriers equals high risk; the same threat in a desert is low risk.
  • Attack surface — every possible way in. More doors means more to defend, which is why "reduce the attack surface" is a constant refrain.

Social engineering

Manipulating people rather than machines. It remains the most reliable way in, because people are patchable only through training and training expires.

TypeWhat it is
PhishingMass fraudulent emails hoping someone bites
Spear phishingTargeted at a specific person, using real details about them
WhalingSpear phishing aimed at executives, who approve large payments
Vishing / smishingThe same thing by voice call or SMS
Business email compromiseImpersonating a trusted colleague or supplier to trigger a transfer
Watering holeCompromising a site the target group already visits, and waiting
USB baitingLeaving infected drives where curious people will find and plug them in
TailgatingWalking in behind someone who badged through a door

Malware

  • Virus — attaches to a file and needs a human to run it
  • Worm — spreads by itself across a network, no human required (the Morris worm)
  • Ransomware — encrypts your files and demands payment; an attack on availability
  • Spyware — hides and quietly collects information; an attack on confidentiality
  • Trojan — disguised as something legitimate so you install it yourself

Web-based attacks

SQL injection — feeding database commands into an input box that expected a name, because the application pastes your text straight into a query. Cross-site scripting (XSS) — getting your malicious script stored on a site so it runs in other visitors' browsers. Both come from the same root cause: trusting input you didn't generate.

From my own lab

In the DVWA assessment I ran, the critical finding wasn't an injection or an exploit. It was a directory listing left enabled plus a config.inc.php.bak file nobody deleted. Because the server won't execute .bak as PHP, it served the source as plain text — database username and password included, to anyone with a browser. Two boring misconfigurations, chained, equals full database access.

Part 05Why smart people click bad links

The most useful thing in Course 1, to my mind. Social engineering works by exploiting specific psychological levers, and once you can name them you start noticing them in your own inbox.

  • Authority — the message appears to come from your CEO, the tax office, IT support. We're trained to comply with authority quickly.
  • Urgency — "your account will be closed in 24 hours." Panic suppresses careful thinking, which is the entire point.
  • Intimidation — mild threats of consequences: a fine, a suspension, trouble with your manager.
  • Social proof — "everyone on your team has already completed this."
  • Scarcity — a limited offer, a last chance, only three left.
  • Familiarity — the attacker has done homework and mentions your actual colleagues or recent projects.
  • Trust — a rapport built over several harmless messages before the real ask arrives.
The one habit worth building

Notice when a message is making you feel rushed. That feeling is the attack. Legitimate organisations are generally fine with you taking ten minutes to verify through a channel you chose yourself — a phone number you looked up, not the one in the email. Urgency is the single most common ingredient, and slowing down defeats most of it.

Part 06The eight security domains

Security is wide. These eight domains (from the CISSP framework) are how the field divides itself up — useful mainly for working out which part you want to live in.

#DomainIn plain terms
1Security & Risk ManagementPolicies, compliance, deciding which risks are worth accepting
2Asset SecurityKnowing what you own and classifying how sensitive it is
3Security Architecture & EngineeringDesigning systems that are secure by construction
4Communication & Network SecuritySecuring data in transit — firewalls, VPNs, segmentation
5Identity & Access ManagementWho are you, and what exactly are you allowed to touch
6Security Assessment & TestingAudits, vulnerability scanning, penetration testing
7Security OperationsDay-to-day monitoring, detection, incident response — the SOC
8Software Development SecurityBuilding security into code rather than bolting it on

Most entry-level analyst roles sit in domain 7, with a foot in domain 6. My own lab work — scanning, enumerating, assessing, reporting — is squarely domain 6.

Part 07Frameworks and controls

A framework is the plan; controls are the specific things you implement. Framework says "protect sensitive data"; a control is "enforce multi-factor authentication."

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework

Five functions, and they double as a decent mental checklist for any system:

01

Identify

what you have

02

Protect

put up defences

03

Detect

notice trouble

04

Respond

contain it

…followed by Recover — restoring normal operations and learning from it. The order matters: you cannot protect what you haven't identified, and most organisations are weakest at Detect, which is precisely why SOC analysts get hired.

Three control types

  • Administrative — policies, training, procedures (people)
  • Technical — firewalls, encryption, MFA (technology)
  • Physical — locks, badges, cameras (the building)

Principles worth memorising

  • Least privilege — give the minimum access needed to do the job, and no more. If an intern's account is compromised, it should unlock very little.
  • Defense in depth — layers, so one failure isn't total failure. A castle has a moat, walls, and guards.
  • Separation of duties — no single person can complete a sensitive action alone. The person who requests a payment shouldn't approve it. (Accountants have known this one for centuries.)
  • Minimise the attack surface — every feature, port and account you don't need is one you also have to defend.
  • Keep security simple — complexity creates gaps nobody notices.

Part 08What a security analyst does all day

Less dramatic than the films, and more interesting than it sounds. An entry-level analyst typically:

  • Monitors systems and alerts for anything abnormal
  • Triages those alerts — the core skill, and mostly a matter of deciding what's a real threat versus noise
  • Investigates the ones that survive triage, gathering evidence before escalating
  • Follows playbooks — documented procedures for handling a given incident type
  • Supports audits and checks that controls are actually working
  • Documents everything, because an incident with no record can't be learned from

The tools

  • SIEM (Splunk, Chronicle) — aggregates logs from everywhere so you can search them in one place and get alerted on patterns
  • IDS/IPS — intrusion detection and prevention; one warns, the other blocks
  • Packet analysers (Wireshark, tcpdump) — read the actual traffic on the wire
  • Network scanners (Nmap) — discover what's running and exposed
  • Linux, SQL and Python — the connective tissue: navigating systems, querying logs, automating the repetitive parts
Why I think I can do this

Alert triage is pattern recognition against a baseline — deciding which anomaly is real. I did the same thing with data for two years. In one audit I flagged an 89.7x return-on-spend figure as implausible against a 2.5–4.7x peer range; it turned out to be a units error that had inflated the headline number by 73%. The instinct that says "that's too good, something's broken" is the same instinct a SOC needs. The domain is new to me. The reflex isn't.

Part 09Ethics: the part that gets skipped

Security people are handed access that would be criminal in other hands. The distinction between a security professional and an attacker is not skill — it's authorisation.

  • Confidentiality — you'll see data you have no business discussing. Don't.
  • Privacy — access granted for one purpose isn't permission for another. Being able to read something is not permission to read it.
  • Law — scanning or testing systems without written permission is a crime in most countries, regardless of intent or whether you caused harm.

This is why every project on this site states its scope explicitly. My Nmap work runs against my own machine, scanme.nmap.org (which exists for exactly this purpose), and platforms like TryHackMe that authorise it. That boundary isn't a formality — a habit of testing "just to see" is how promising careers end before they start.

The only thing separating a penetration tester from a criminal is a signed piece of paper.

Part 10Where to actually start

If this is where you are now, here's the order I'd suggest — and am following myself.

Weeks 1–4

Concepts before tools

The material in this post. You can't interpret a scan result without knowing what ports and protocols are.

  • Google Cybersecurity Certificate Course 1, or any solid free intro
  • Networking basics: IP addresses, ports, TCP/IP, the three-way handshake
Weeks 5–10

Linux and the command line

Almost every security tool assumes it. Get comfortable before you need it under pressure.

  • Navigation, permissions, processes, grep and piping
  • Enough Bash to automate a repeated sequence of commands
Weeks 11–16

Hands-on tools in a lab you own

Where it stops being theory. Build the lab first, then break it.

  • Nmap against scanme.nmap.org and your own machines
  • Wireshark — capture your own traffic and read it
  • A deliberately vulnerable app (DVWA) running locally
  • TryHackMe for guided, authorised practice
Ongoing

Write everything up

The single highest-leverage habit, and the one most beginners skip.

  • Turn each lab into a short report: methodology, findings, severity, remediation
  • Publish them — a written assessment proves more than a certificate does

That last point is the one I'd argue hardest for. Plenty of people can run a scan. Far fewer can explain what the output means, decide which finding matters most, and write it down so a manager can act on it. That gap is where you become employable.

The honest summary

Nothing in Course 1 makes you a security professional. What it does is give you the vocabulary and the mental model, so that when you finally run a scan the output means something instead of looking like noise.

The field is far less about exotic hacking than it looks from outside. It's mostly about promises — confidentiality, integrity, availability — and the unglamorous, repetitive discipline of keeping them. The Equifax breach happened because of an unapplied patch. Most of them do.
